Where free drug samples actually move prescriptions

An econometric study on physician-level data from one mature market and two product launches. It separates the real effect of samples from the sales force's targeting bias, and turns the estimated response into a budget-neutral allocation plan.

The problem

A large budget, allocated without evidence

Free samples are one of the largest commercial investments a pharmaceutical company makes to influence prescribing. Yet their allocation typically follows rep intuition and account history, not measured response.

The obvious comparison — doctors who received samples versus those who didn't — shows a 17-point share gap. It looks like a clear win. It isn't, and working out why is the core of this project.

Study design: contrasting cases

Rather than one average effect across a whole portfolio — an estimate that describes no market in particular — the analysis focuses on three markets chosen by method, not convenience.

The central issue

Reps don't visit doctors at random

Sales representatives go back to doctors who already prescribe more. Those doctors would show a higher share even with no samples at all. A naive model credits that pre-existing advantage to the samples.

The fix is to estimate the same coefficient under increasing control, and watch what survives.

How fixed effects work

For every doctor, each monthly value is replaced by its deviation from that doctor's own average. Specialty, hospital, patient mix, relationship with the rep — anything constant — appears in both the value and the average, so it cancels. Including what was never measured. Replicated by hand: demeaning and running plain OLS gives +0.540, matching the model's +0.544.

A second, independent check

Propensity score matching builds a comparison group from doctors who looked alike before treatment. It removes 46% of the raw gap. An earlier version reported only 1.2% — because it matched on same-period prescription volume, a variable the treatment itself changes.

What the samples actually do

Conversion in mature markets, adoption in launches

A share model can't distinguish two very different mechanisms: taking prescriptions from competitors, or growing the whole category. So the effect was decomposed within each doctor into own prescriptions and competitor prescriptions.

A correction worth showing

A pooled count model first suggested visits raise prescription volume by 9–19%. Comparing each doctor with himself, the effect disappears in the mature market. The pooled estimate was picking up the same bias as before: reps visit high-volume doctors, so volume attracts visits rather than the other way round.

Continuity beats intensity

Three-month accumulated exposure outperforms same-month delivery in every market and every specification (+0.675 vs. +0.544 pp under fixed effects): the effect carries over. At equal total volume, spreading deliveries across more months associates with +1.03 pp per extra month — about the same as 17 additional units.

And it saturates

Launches: the event study

From estimate to decision

The most responsive doctors get the least

Splitting the response by each doctor's prior share reveals the result that matters most: doctors who barely prescribed the product respond 2.5 times more — they have room to convert. A doctor already at 80% share can't gain 20 points. Current allocation does the opposite.

The allocation model

  • Objective: maximize incremental prescriptions, not share points — a point on a high-volume doctor is worth more
  • Response: quadratic function estimated separately per prior-share tercile
  • Budget: units allocated equal units actually delivered — cost-neutral
  • Dose cap: no doctor above his saturation point
  • Ceiling: gains can't exceed the room left up to 100% share
  • Solver: greedy assignment over 0.5-unit increments, sorted by marginal gain

What the model does not include

Coverage falls from 5,909 to 3,659 doctors, and the relationship cost of that is not in the model. Response is assumed homogeneous within each tercile. It is a prioritization tool — the recommendation is a controlled pilot before committing the full budget.

Is specialty a better segmenter?

It's the usual commercial criterion, so it was tested. Only 2 of 15 specialties show a significant response. The apparently large effects belong to small groups with confidence intervals too wide to read. Prior share supports a policy; specialty is useful only as a secondary reach criterion.

How it was built

From three disconnected sources to one panel

Prescriptions came from an external audit, samples and visits from the CRM — with no shared key. The CRM identifies doctors only by name. The bridge was the commercial physician master file, the only table with name, license number and territory together.

Matching license numbers against the audit's graduation license alone linked 47% of doctors. Accepting the specialty license as well raised it to 88%. Final coverage: 97.2% of sample records and 94.8% of visits.

Two decisions that changed the results

Keep the zeros. In Market B only 36.8% of doctors who received samples ever prescribed in that market. Dropping months with no prescriptions removes exactly the cases where samples didn't work.

Use the lost history. The sample record starts in March 2025 after a CRM migration, but prescription data goes back 19 more months. Instead of discarding it, it became each doctor's baseline — the control that raised R² from 0.06 to 0.29.

Limits

What this can and can't claim

Supported
  • Samples have a positive, significant effect after strict controls
  • In the mature market that effect is conversion from competitors
  • Returns diminish and response varies strongly by prior share
  • The naive estimate is roughly double the credible one
Not claimed
  • Strict causality — the data is observational
  • That spreading deliveries causes gains; that evidence is cross-sectional
  • An event study for the mature market — left-censored by the CRM migration
  • Transferability to markets with different competitive dynamics

The proper next step is a randomized pilot: comparable doctors, same total volume, assigned to allocation schemes by lot for three to four cycles. That turns a well-founded hypothesis into a verified result.
