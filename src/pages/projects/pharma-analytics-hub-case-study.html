The problem

A process that grew by accumulation

Every month, the commercial team of a global pharmaceutical company receives market audits from an external vendor. They feed the reports used by the sales force, marketing and leadership. The process lived in notebooks and scripts, and the order of execution lived in one person's head.

  • Each audit named the same product differentlyReports never reconciled
  • Joins were done by nameA rename broke them silently
  • Validation meant eyeballingErrors surfaced after sending
  • 6–8 hours per cycleA workday a month on mechanics

Architecture

Three layers, not five

I started with the full medallion pattern and removed the layers that didn't separate anything real. The boundary that matters here isn't bronze/silver/gold — it's computed (CSV anyone can open and audit) versus delivered (formatted Excel for the end user).

Landing

Files as received. Never edited.

→

Computed

Deliverables

Evidence

Validations and findings.

Join by code, never by name

The same product arrives spelled three ways depending on the audit, but with the same code. Several historical errors came from name joins that broke silently after a rename.

Catalogs as the single source of truth

Official names, aliases and composite markets live in tables the business edits. A new consolidated market is one row, not a code change.

Derive instead of capture

Statuses that used to be maintained by hand in Excel are now computed every month. What is captured goes stale; what is derived does not.

History instead of recalculation

Commercial weights change month to month. Each period's weights are stored, so a past month reproduces exactly as it was published.

Severity-aware validation

It tells apart a market that is truly missing from one spelled differently in another source — two problems with different fixes that used to look identical.

Documentation that never goes stale

The layout of every input and output is generated from the real files and is stable across months. If it changes, a vendor changed the format.

What it caught

Errors that produced perfectly believable numbers

  • Silent

    Two columns published as zero for months — a default value hid that the field was never read.

  • Silent

    Duplicated quotas summed without warning, inflating targets and sinking reported attainment.

  • Double count

    A consolidated market counted twice alongside its own components.

  • Broken join

    Packs that stopped matching their market after a product rename.

  • Environment

    Code that worked on one machine and failed on another due to library version differences.

Lessons

A normal-looking number isn't validated.

The costliest errors raised no alerts.

Validate against the raw source.

Matching the old process only proves you copied its bugs.

Fewer layers, fewer exceptions.

Every simplification made it easier to run for someone who didn't build it.
